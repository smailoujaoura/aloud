The lighthouse keeper's log

Every evening at dusk, the keeper climbed the spiral stairs to light the lamp. He wrote the weather in a ledger that nobody else would ever read. Wind from the west, visibility good, two boats heading north. The entries are short, but together they make a kind of diary of the sea.

The quiet economics of lighthouses

For most of the nineteenth century, a lighthouse was the most expensive thing a coastal town would ever build. The stone came by sea, and the lens came from Paris.

Economists later used the lighthouse as their favourite example of something no market would provide. A ship could not be charged for seeing a light, so nobody would ever pay to build one.

In England, private owners did build lights, and collected dues from ships at the nearest port. It worked for two hundred years.

    Bell Rock Lighthouse[edit]

    Location: North Sea, off Angus
    Built: 1807 to 1810
    Height: 35 m

    The Bell Rock Lighthouse is the oldest surviving sea-washed lighthouse in the world.[1] It was built between 1807 and 1810 on a reef in the North Sea, eleven miles off the coast of Scotland.[2][3]

    1 Introduction

    Lighthouses were financed by dues collected at port, set by tonnage and by the number of lights a vessel passed. The scheme survived for two centuries, long after its critics said it could not work. This paper follows the ledgers of three stations.
