Chrome and Firefox do equally well: what matters is whether the voice can reach your graphics card through WebGPU. Chrome has it on already. Firefox keeps it switched off for now, and it takes a minute to turn on. Aloud's settings test your machine and tell you which case you're in.

Turn on WebGPU in Firefox
  1. Open a new tab, type about:config into the address bar, and accept the warning.
  2. Search for dom.webgpu.enabled and set it to true. This turns WebGPU on.
  3. Search for dom.webgpu.workers.enabled and set it to true. This is the one that matters: Aloud makes its voice in a background worker, and this lets the worker use the graphics card.
  4. Only if hardware acceleration is off on your machine: set gfx.webrender.all to true.
  5. Reload the page you're reading. Aloud's settings should now say the voice runs on your graphics card; if they don't, restart Firefox.

To undo it, search for the same names and click the reset arrow next to each.

On a real page

It reads where the article is, without breaking it.

No copy-and-paste, no separate reader window. Open an article, press Alt+R, and Aloud follows along on the page itself.

The sentence, lit up

What's being read is highlighted where it sits. Scroll away, and one button brings you back to it.

Your highlights, kept

Select, press K. While Aloud reads, your marks turn into underlines so the two never clash.

A bar that stays put

Play, skip, speed, voice, save as MP3. It tucks away when you scroll and never jumps under your pointer.

Notes beside the text

Press Alt+N for this page's notes, or search all of them. Each one is a click from its passage.

Settings that explain themselves

Every voice has a face, a size and a preview.

Choose by listening, not by reading model names. Aloud shows what each voice costs to download, what's already on this device, and how fast it reads in words a minute rather than a mysterious “1.4×”.

Why Aloud

Natural voices used to mean sending your reading to someone's server.

Good text-to-speech has lived in the cloud: you upload the article, they send back audio, and the best voices sit behind a monthly plan. Aloud runs the same kind of neural model on your own machine.

Features

Built for reading long things, on pages you don't control.

Word by word

The sentence is shaded and the word being spoken marked inside it, timed from the voice's own pronunciation.

No gaps, no waiting

Sentences are made a few ahead of the one you're hearing and played back to back. Uses your graphics card when it can.

Picks up where you stopped

Come back tomorrow and land on the exact sentence, checked against the text so an edited page never resumes in the wrong place.

PDFs, properly

Real pages with figures and columns intact, the sentence highlighted on the page. Mark up, comment, and save the marks into the PDF.

Highlights and notes

Press K to keep a passage in your colour. Marks are anchored to the text, so they survive the page changing. Export to Markdown.

A reading queue

Save articles for later and Aloud reads them one after another. Continue, Up next, Notes and History, all in one place.

Save as MP3

Turn a whole article or PDF into one audio file, in your voice and speed, for a walk or a commute. There's a sleep timer too.

Drifted off?

One key goes back about ten seconds and shows you where that is on the page. Speed shows real words a minute.

Every video, too New

The same speed keys for any video or audio player on the web, remembered per site. Rewind and skip, even on YouTube Shorts. And it can stop videos playing by themselves.

Private by construction

The voice lives on your device. So does everything you read.

Not a policy, an architecture: there is no Aloud server to send anything to. Here is the complete list of what touches the network.

Never leaves this device

  • The speech itself: every sentence is made locally
  • Your highlights and notes, and the tags on them
  • Where you stopped in every article and PDF
  • Your reading queue and history
  • Settings, keys, colours and per-site video speeds

Goes out only when you ask

  • Downloading a voice, once, from Hugging Face
  • Previewing a Piper voice: a short hosted clip
  • Fetching a page or PDF you told Aloud to read or queue

A saved article's images stay unfetched until you press Load images, so reopening one tells nobody you're reading it. Aloud only runs on a site when you start it there, or on sites you list yourself.

How it's built

A speech engine, an audio scheduler and a PDF reader, inside a browser extension.

Aloud is a TypeScript extension with no backend. From a cluttered web page to a spoken, highlighted sentence, every step runs on your machine.

  1. Content script

    Extract

    Mozilla Readability strips navigation and ads and keeps the article.

  2. Content script

    Plan

    The text is normalised, split into sentences and mapped back to its exact place on the page.

  3. Worker pool

    Synthesise

    Neural voices run on ONNX Runtime Web: WebGPU where the browser allows it, parallel WebAssembly workers everywhere else.

  4. Web Audio

    Play and follow

    Audio is scheduled sample-accurately, back to back, while the sentence and word are highlighted in place.

Three neural engines in a tab

Piper (VITS), Kokoro-82M (StyleTTS 2) and Supertonic-2, each with its own phonemiser and model format, behind one engine interface.

Highlights that can't break the page

The CSS Custom Highlight API marks text without changing the page's DOM. Notes are anchored by their text, so they find their place again after the page is edited.

One codebase, three browsers

Firefox, Firefox for Android and Chromium from one Manifest V3 codebase built with WXT. Differences are detected as capabilities, not guessed from the browser's name.

Isolated from the page

Synthesis runs in a hidden extension frame that only accepts connections carrying a one-time ticket from the extension, so a hostile page can't drive it.

Safe with many tabs open

Every saved change is serialised through a lock in the background, so two tabs saving notes at once can't silently overwrite each other.

Tested by ear

Over 300 automated tests, and every engine change is checked by transcribing its audio with speech recognition. The samples on this page were made the same way.
